# EPAI: Embodied Physical Agentic Intelligence

One ecosystem. Across borders. In your language.

At an ITU meeting on Embodied AI in Hangzhou, I actively participated in WG5's discussions on Connectivity & Interoperability. We were discussing how different AI systems, machines and networks could be connected and work together.

During a conversation with Chinese AI experts, I used an expression I was familiar with: Physical AI. A few of them found the term unfamiliar.

I was surprised. In Korea, I had often heard it used by government and business leaders discussing the future of robotics and manufacturing. Then I remembered my own first encounter with the Chinese term 具身智能 (Embodied AI). I needed it explained before I understood the field it referred to.

If people working in these fields need time to explain their terms to one another, what happens to those who have to invest in the technology and make policy decisions?

One ecosystem.

In China, I often heard Embodied AI. In Korea, Physical AI was familiar to government and business. In the American technology and finance conversations I encountered, Agentic AI came up frequently.

The three expressions draw attention to different things. Embodied AI considers how intelligence learns and acts through a body and its environment. Physical AI focuses on intelligence operating in the physical world, in places such as factories and on roads. Agentic AI focuses on AI using software and tools to pursue goals and carry out work.

These names are familiar to the researchers and companies in each field. Yet within a single business, technologies described by different names may be put to work on the same task.

Consider a food distributor's warehouse.

When a customer places an order, software checks inventory and delivery schedules. In the warehouse, a robot finds and moves the goods. The robot must perceive its surroundings and exchange information with the systems that manage orders and stock. When a delivery schedule changes or an item cannot be found, an employee steps in to resolve the problem.

To choose equipment and software for the warehouse, management receives proposals from several companies. One leads with Physical AI; another presents Embodied AI. A company offering to automate order processing talks about Agentic AI.

A bank considers financing the equipment, while an insurer examines liability if something goes wrong. After hearing each company, management must decide how these technologies will work together in the warehouse, whether they will help deliver goods on time, and whether the business can afford them.

I propose EPAI as a common name for discussing technologies and industries that are developing under different names.

EPAI stands for Embodied Physical Agentic Intelligence. It is B2AGI's proposal for examining intelligence and technology together with the industries, people, capital and institutions through which they operate, as one ecosystem.

Across borders.

Imagine that government delegations from Korea and China meet to discuss cooperation in the AI industry.

The Korean representative says, “The applications of Physical AI in China are impressive.”

The Chinese representative asks, “What do you mean by Physical AI?”

The Korean representative describes intelligent machines used in factories and logistics. The Chinese representative asks whether this relates to Embodied AI.

Now the Korean representative has a question: “Embodied AI? Is that the same as Physical AI?”

Both sides explain the scope of the terms they use and look at policy documents and company examples. They then discuss where they might cooperate, which companies and research institutions to bring together, and who would invest in and operate the projects.

If they use EPAI as a common agenda while preparing for the meeting, both sides can place their materials together. They can identify what China's Embodied AI and Korea's Physical AI each cover, then find the sites and companies that interest them both.

Keeping a record of the discussion and the scope of the terms gives them something to bring to their next meeting. It also makes it easier to bring new participants into the conversation.

In your language.

Now consider a mayor preparing a city for the AI era.

The mayor wants local businesses to make use of new technology and wants universities and companies to find opportunities to work together. An adviser is asked to research examples from other countries.

The materials include Korean Physical AI policies and company announcements. Chinese sources discuss Embodied AI. American companies and investment reports refer to Agentic AI.

The adviser presents the terms and examples in turn: what Physical AI means, what Embodied AI covers, and what services Agentic AI companies are developing.

The mayor asks, “Are these the same thing, or are they different?”

The adviser explains where the terms differ and overlap. Then the mayor asks, “What should our city do?”

The mayor wants to know which industry to start with and where to allocate the budget. The adviser wants to prepare a report that answers that question, but the sources use different names and categories. Where should the explanation begin?

Using EPAI as a common heading, the adviser could start this way:

“Mayor, today's report is on EPAI. We have looked at China's Embodied AI, what Korea calls Physical AI, and Agentic AI in software. I will explain how these technologies could be used in our city's industries and services.”

The adviser then proposes preparing a site where local logistics companies can test new systems. University researchers and developers could take part, with businesses in the industrial district joining the trials. If overseas companies are invited, the city will also need to settle operating costs, access to site data and security conditions.

The adviser has brought materials from several countries into one report. The mayor can use it to discuss which industrial district to start in and which companies to meet first. When a technical distinction matters to the decision, a specialist can explain it.

A similar question arises in an investment meeting.

Imagine an investment committee receiving three proposals: one from an Embodied AI company, one from a Physical AI company and one from an Agentic AI company.

Each company includes a market forecast in its investment materials. Because the markets are named and defined differently, the committee first needs to understand how much the three figures overlap. Adding them together could count the same customers and business more than once.

One committee member asks, “Are these companies competitors? Or might a single customer need them together?”

With EPAI as a common heading, the committee can follow the work of the food distributor described earlier.

Which company provides the software that processes orders? Whose machine moves the goods through the warehouse? Who connects the machines and software, and who responds when something goes wrong?

The comparison may reveal companies selling similar functions. It may also show that companies thought to be in entirely different markets are needed together to complete the same customer's work. Connecting and operating their products may itself offer an investment opportunity.

Seeing the elephant together

There is an old story about people touching different parts of an elephant.

The person touching the trunk describes the trunk. The person touching a leg describes the leg. The person touching the body gives another account. Each is describing something they have experienced. But without knowing they are standing before the same elephant, they struggle to understand what the others are talking about.

I think something similar is happening in conversations about AI. Researchers, manufacturers, software companies, investors and governments are each looking at different parts of a changing world, and those parts can be connected.

EPAI is a name I propose for understanding that wider world together, starting with the part each of us knows. It describes an ecosystem of intelligence and technology, people and industry, capital and institutions.

Chinese researchers can continue to use Embodied AI. Korean manufacturers can speak of Physical AI, and software companies can discuss Agentic AI. Each can use the language that best describes their work.

When they meet to build a business or discuss investment and policy, they can also look at where their knowledge belongs in the same ecosystem.

There is more to gain from that encounter than an exchange of what each person already knows. A researcher who hears about a problem on the ground may develop a new technology. An operator who encounters technology from another field may start a business they had not considered before. The experience they build together can open another possibility when they meet again.

At B2AGI, I am working on EPAI 2027 and the B2AGI World Atlas. I hope others will spend less time working through the confusion I experienced in Hangzhou, and more time discussing what they can build together and where they can invest.
